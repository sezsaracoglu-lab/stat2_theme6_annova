One-Way ANOVA — comparing more than two means

Lecture 6 · Course 2.1 Methods & Statistics · Bruno Bocanegra · Erasmus University Rotterdam

A note before you start

Sezen — this lecture is where your statistics course stops being about two things and starts being about many. Everything you have done so far compares a pair: this group against that one, before against after. The moment a question has three answers instead of two, the t test runs out, and the one-factor ANOVA takes over.

Here is why I think it is worth more than the marks. You spend your working life comparing groups: engagement across eight countries, promotion rates across several populations, scores from three different assessment centres. The instinct is to eyeball the averages and declare a winner. This lecture teaches the discipline that stops you: before you call a gap real, you have to know how much people differ ANYWAY, inside each group. That ratio — gap against ordinary variation — is the whole of F, and it is the single most transferable idea in the course.

It also teaches you where to stop. A significant F buys you one modest sentence: at least one group differs. It does not tell you which, it does not tell you how much, and it never tells you why. Three separate ideas, three separate steps — and the exam tests the seams between them.

How to use this page: read the Overview once, then take the lessons in order. Each one opens with the diagram, because that is the part you will remember under exam pressure. Do the comprehension checks before you look at the answers, play with the demos until the numbers stop surprising you, then use the flashcards and the MCQ to find out what has actually stuck.

🏛️ The argument in one paragraph

Here is the single thesis that ties the whole lecture together: a difference between group means only means something relative to how much people differ inside the groups. Everything else is bookkeeping in the service of that one comparison. The total spread is split in two — between the group means, and within the groups — because the within piece is the one the factor cannot touch, which makes it an honest yardstick. Each piece is divided by its own degrees of freedom so that two sums built from different amounts of data become comparable averages, and the ratio of those averages is F. Under H₀ both estimate the same population variance, so F sits near 1; a large F says the means are further apart than the noise allows, and the table turns it into a p-value. What you win is deliberately small — at least one population mean differs — so R² is reported alongside to say how much of the spread the factor accounts for, post-hoc tests with a corrected alpha are needed to say which pairs differ, and causality is settled by the design rather than by the size of F. Learn the ratio and the rest of the lecture is detail.

🧭 Where the ANOVA sits — and why it exists

Why three groups need a new test

You already know the independent-samples t test: two groups, one quantitative score, and a question about whether the two means differ. The one-factor ANOVA is that same question asked of three groups, or four, or ten.

  • 📏The DEPENDENT variable is the score: quantitative, one number per person.
  • 🏷️The INDEPENDENT variable is the factor: qualitative, and it says which group someone is in.
  • 🧍🧍Two groups → an independent-samples t test does the job.
  • 🧍🧍🧍Three or more groups → one-factor ANOVA, which tests all of them in a single F test.
  • ⚠️The obvious alternative — just run a t test on every pair — is exactly what the last part of this lecture explains you must not do without a correction.
🪝 Memory hook
ANOVA is the t test that learned to count past two.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“I want to compare the average score across several groups at once. A t test only handles two groups, so for three or more I use a one-factor ANOVA — same kind of data, one test instead of several.”
★ Key insight
Nothing about the DATA changes when you move from a t test to an ANOVA. The dependent variable is still quantitative and the independent variable is still categorical. Only the number of levels grows.
🧪 Demo 1 — which test does it call for?
Pick one. You will get the reasoning either way.
🎬 Watch: Where the ANOVA sits — and why it exists🎬 Watch: a worked example
🧩 Comprehension check
A researcher measures reaction time (in ms) for people who drank coffee, tea or water. Which test, and why?
One-factor ANOVA. Reaction time is quantitative — that is the dependent variable — and drink type is a qualitative factor with three levels. Three groups rules out the two-sample t test.
And if there had been only coffee and water?
Two groups, so an independent-samples t test. An ANOVA would also be valid and, against the pooled-variance two-sided t test, would give F = t² — the same conclusion either way.

🏷️ Factor, levels, dependent variable — naming the design

Factor, levels, dependent variable — and where k and N come from

Almost every exam question begins by making you name the parts. Get these three names right and the rest of the question usually falls out of them, because k comes from the levels and N comes from the scores.

  • 🏷️FACTOR — the qualitative variable that groups people. “Type of study”, “political party”, “school type”, “sound”.
  • 🪜LEVELS — the categories of that factor. Psychology, Pedagogy, Sociology, Administration are four levels of ONE factor, not four factors.
  • 🔢k — the number of levels. It is the only thing about the factor that the arithmetic needs.
  • 📐DEPENDENT VARIABLE — the quantitative measurement: kilometres driven, social intelligence, spatial awareness score.
  • 🧮N — the total number of scores across all the groups, not the number per group.
k = number of levels of the factor · N = total number of scores
🪝 Memory hook
One factor, many levels. The factor is the question; the levels are the answers people gave.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“The factor is what I split people by. The levels are the categories I split them into. The dependent variable is the number I measured on each of them.”
★ Key insight
The dependent variable is never a level, and the levels are never separate factors. “One-way” literally means one factor — however many levels it has.
🎬 Watch: Factor, levels, dependent variable — naming the design🎬 Watch: a worked example
🧩 Comprehension check
“Do students from Regular, Montessori, Jenaplan and Dalton schools differ in social intelligence?” Name the factor, the levels, k and the dependent variable.
Factor: school type. Levels: Regular, Montessori, Jenaplan, Dalton — so k = 4. Dependent variable: social intelligence, which is the quantitative score. N would be all the students across the four schools combined.
A classmate says “there are four factors here, one per school type”. What has gone wrong?
They have confused levels with factors. There is one factor (school type) that takes four values. If there were genuinely four factors you would need a four-way ANOVA, which is a different analysis entirely.

🎧 The Mozart study — the example behind every number today

Fifty-seven people, three sounds, one score each

The lecture runs one study from start to finish. Every figure you will meet — 561.41, 1883.96, 8.05, .23 — comes from this one experiment, so it is worth knowing it cold.

  • ❓Research question: does the kind of sound you listen to change your spatial awareness score?
  • 🧍Between-subject design: each participant belongs to exactly ONE group and gives exactly ONE measurement.
  • 🎧Factor: sound, with 3 levels — Mozart, Michael Jackson, Weather report. So k = 3.
  • 📐Dependent variable: spatial awareness score, quantitative.
  • 🔢57 subjects randomly assigned: 19, 20 and 18 of them. So N = 57.
🪝 Memory hook
19 + 20 + 18 = 57, and three sounds. N and k, before anything else.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“They played three different things to three groups of people and then measured how well each person did on a spatial task. The question is whether the kind of sound made a difference to the average score.”
★ Key insight
Random assignment is not decoration. It is the thing that will let us make a CAUSAL claim at the end of the lecture — and it is the thing the school-type example does not have.
⚠ A note on these numbers

The lecture computes SS Between from the ONE-DECIMAL means (20.8, 20.7, 14.0) with an overall mean of 18.7, and that is what reproduces 561.41. 🧩 Comprehension check
What are N and k for this study, and where did you get each?
N = 57, by adding the three group sizes (19 + 20 + 18). k = 3, by counting the levels of the factor “sound”. N comes from the people; k comes from the factor.
Why is this called a between-subject design?
Because the comparison runs BETWEEN different people: each person is in one group only. If the same 19 people had heard all three sounds, that would be a within-subject design and would need a different analysis.

⚖️ Why comparing means is called “analysis of VARIANCE”

Why a test about means is called analysis of variance

The name looks like a mistake. You want to compare MEANS, and the test is called analysis of VARIANCE. The name is exactly right, and seeing why is the single most useful thing in this lecture.

  • 🔀There are three spreads you can measure: of all the scores, of the scores inside each group, and of the group means.
  • 🎯BETWEEN-group variation: systematic differences caused by the independent variable. If the factor does something, this is where it shows up.
  • 🔊WITHIN-group variation: error, noise, individual differences between subjects. Everyone in a group got the same treatment, so whatever still varies inside a group has nothing to do with the factor.
  • ⚖️The within part is therefore a clean measure of noise — a yardstick the factor cannot touch.
  • ➕So: compare how far apart the group MEANS are against how noisy people are INSIDE the groups. Means, judged by variances.
SS Total = SS Between + SS Within
🪝 Memory hook
Signal and noise. Between is the signal, within is the noise, and F is signal ÷ noise.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“Group averages are always a bit different from each other, even when nothing is going on, just from random variation. So you measure how much people vary inside a group — that is pure noise — and ask whether the group averages are further apart than that noise can explain.”
★ Key insight
You cannot judge whether two means are far apart until you know how noisy the data are. A gap of 6 points is enormous if people inside a group vary by 1, and nothing at all if they vary by 20.
🎬 Watch: Why comparing means is called “analysis of VARIANCE”🎬 Watch: a worked example
🧩 Comprehension check
Why does the within-group variation count as “error” even though the people are perfectly real?
Because everyone inside a group received the same level of the factor. Any difference still showing up between them cannot be caused by the factor — it is individual variation, which is what “error” means here. It is not a mistake by the researcher.
If the factor has a very strong effect, which of the three sums of squares grows?
SS Between. It is built from how far the group means sit from the overall mean, so pushing the groups apart inflates it. SS Within is untouched by the factor, which is exactly why it makes a fair yardstick.
Lecture 5 of 16 · hypotheses · slides 26

🎯 The two hypotheses, which never change

H₀ and Hₐ, in the only form they ever take

For every one-factor ANOVA you will ever meet, the hypotheses have the same shape. They are about the POPULATION means — the μ’s — never about the sample means you can see in front of you.

  • 0️⃣H₀: μ₁ = μ₂ = … = μₖ. All the population means are equal.
  • 🅰️Hₐ: in the population, at least one mean is different from the others. Equivalently: H₀ is not true.
  • ⚖️Exactly one of the two is true. The test chooses between them.
  • 🚫Hₐ is never directional. There is no “higher”, no “better”, no one-sided version.
  • 🚫Hₐ is never “all the means differ”. One mean out of ten standing apart already makes H₀ false.
H₀: μ_Mozart = μ_Michael = μ_Weather Hₐ: at least one differs
🪝 Memory hook
Not-all-equal. Three words, and they are the whole alternative hypothesis.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“The null says the groups are the same on average in the population. The alternative just says that is wrong — at least one of them is out of line. It does not say which one or in which direction.”
★ Key insight
The exam loves this. Any option containing “higher”, “lower”, “only one” or “all of them differ” can be struck out before you read the rest of it.
🎬 Watch: The two hypotheses, which never change🎬 Watch: a worked example
🧩 Comprehension check
A study compares three fertilisers and expects fertiliser C to be best. Can Hₐ say “C gives the highest yield”?
No. That is a directional claim about one specific group, and F cannot test it — squaring the deviations throws the direction away. Hₐ stays “not all three population means are equal”. Finding out that C is the best one is a post-hoc question, covered in lesson 15.
Why are the hypotheses written with μ rather than x̄?
Because hypotheses are claims about populations. The sample means x̄ are already known — you measured them — so there is nothing to hypothesise about.

✂️ Splitting the spread: the three sums of squares

Three sums of squares, and the one line that links them

A sum of squares is exactly what it says: you take a set of distances, square each one, and add them up. All that changes between the three is WHICH distances you take.

  • 🟨SS Total — every score against the OVERALL mean. The variation in all the scores.
  • 🟩SS Between — every GROUP MEAN against the overall mean, each one weighted by how many people are in that group. Defined as the variation you get when you replace each score by its group mean.
  • 🟦SS Within — every score against ITS OWN group mean. The variation left inside the groups.
  • 📦When you are given group variances rather than raw scores, SS Within = Σ(nᵢ − 1)sᵢ² — the same quantity, from summary data.
  • ⚖️The weighting by group size in SS Between is not optional: a mean from 20 people carries more weight than a mean from 5.
SS Between = Σ nᵢ(x̄ᵢ − x̄)² SS Within = Σ(score − x̄ᵢ)² = Σ(nᵢ − 1)sᵢ² SS Total = SS Between + SS Within
🪝 Memory hook
Between uses the group MEANS. Within uses the SCORES. Total uses both, and is just the two added together.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“I measure how far the group averages sit from the overall average — that is one pile. Then how far each person sits from their own group’s average — that is the other pile. Add the two piles and you have the total spread.”
★ Key insight
Because the two pieces add to the total, any exam question that gives you two of the three is really a subtraction question. That is the single most common ANOVA-table item there is.

An ANOVA reports SS Total = 410.94 and SS Within = 253.50. What is SS Between?

🧮 Degrees of freedom — and the check that costs two seconds

Count the groups, count the people, subtract once each

Sums of squares are not comparable to each other, because a big sum can simply mean many terms were added. To make them comparable, each one has to be divided by its own degrees of freedom — so first you need those.

  • 🔢df Between = k − 1. It is built from the k group means, and you lose one because the overall mean is fixed. This is the NUMERATOR df of F.
  • 🟩df Within = N − k. It is built from all N scores, and you lose one per group because each group’s own mean was estimated from its own data. This is the DENOMINATOR df.
  • 🟦df Total = N − 1.
  • 🟪And they add: (k − 1) + (N − k) = N − 1.
  • ✅For the Mozart study: 2, 54 and 56 — and 2 + 54 = 56. ✓
df Between = k − 1 · df Within = N − k · df Total = N − 1
🪝 Memory hook
Count the groups, count the people, subtract once each. Then check they add up.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“Degrees of freedom are how many numbers were genuinely free to vary. Every average you work out pins one of them down, so you subtract one for each average you used.”
★ Key insight
The denominator df uses EVERYONE (N − k), not the size of one group. Mixing those up is the most common df error in the whole course — and the addition check catches it instantly.

Three groups with 10 observations each. What are the df for the F test?
k = 3 and N = 30, so df Between = 2 and df Within = 30 − 3 = 27. Not 2 and 10: the denominator counts all 30 people. Check: 2 + 27 = 29 = N − 1. ✓

📏 Mean squares — turning a sum into something comparable

Turning totals into averages so they can be compared

A mean square is a rescaled sum of squares — rescaled so that, if H₀ is true, it is a good estimate of the variance in the scores. That last clause is the whole point.

  • ➗MS = SS ÷ df, always on the SAME row. Never cross rows.
  • 🔊MS Within = 1883.96 ÷ 54 = 34.89. This estimates the common population variance, whether or not H₀ is true — the noise is the noise.
  • 🎯MS Between = 561.41 ÷ 2 = 280.71. This estimates the SAME variance, but only IF H₀ is true. If the factor does something, it is inflated by the real differences on top.
  • ⚖️So under H₀ the two are estimating the same thing and their ratio should be about 1. That is the whole reason the ratio is meaningful.
  • 🚫There is no MS Total row: it would be the overall variance, which answers no question in the test.
MS Source = SS Source ⁄ df Source
🪝 Memory hook
A sum of squares is a total; a mean square is an average. Only averages are comparable.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“Adding more numbers always makes a total bigger, so you cannot compare two totals built from different amounts of data. Dividing each by its own count turns them into averages, and then they can be compared.”
★ Key insight
Slide 39 prints MS Between as 286.71. That is a typo: 561.41 ÷ 2 = 280.71, which is what the ANOVA table on slide 45 and the F on slide 41 both use. If a number does not reproduce, do the division yourself.
🎬 Watch: Mean squares — turning a sum into something comparable🎬 Watch: a worked example
🧩 Comprehension check
An ANOVA table shows SS Between = 84 with 3 df and SS Within = 240 with 36 df. What are the two mean squares?
MS Between = 84 ÷ 3 = 28. MS Within = 240 ÷ 36 = 6.67. Each row divided by its own df — never 84 ÷ 36.
MS Within = 12.5 with 36 degrees of freedom. What is SS Within?
12.5 × 36 = 450. The same rule, run backwards — which is exactly what “complete the table” questions ask you to do.
Lecture 9 of 16 · the core idea · slides 34–36

🔬 The logic: what “balanced” looks like when H₀ is true

What H₀ actually predicts, and what it does not

Here is the step that makes the whole test make sense. Suppose H₀ really is true and the three populations have identical means. Will the three SAMPLE means come out identical? Obviously not.

  • 🎲Scores vary. Because scores vary, group means vary too — even when the groups are drawn from identical populations.
  • 📊So between-group variation is never zero, not even under H₀. The question is never “is there any difference?” — there always is.
  • ⚖️Under H₀ the two kinds of variation should be BALANCED: the group means scatter about as much as the noise inside the groups would predict.
  • 🔊Small noise inside the groups → the means should cluster tightly. Large noise → the means can scatter widely and still mean nothing.
  • 📈When H₀ is false, between-group variation is clearly LARGER than within-group variation can account for.
🪝 Memory hook
Even identical populations give different sample means. The test is about HOW different.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“If you measured three groups of people who are really all the same, their averages would still come out slightly different — that is just luck of the draw. So the test asks whether the averages are further apart than luck would explain.”
★ Key insight
This is why the within-group spread has to be in the formula at all. Without it, a gap between means is a number with no scale — you would have no way to say whether it is big.
🧪 Demo 3 — what F is actually comparing
🎬 Watch: The logic: what “balanced” looks like when H₀ is true🎬 Watch: a worked example
🧩 Comprehension check
Two studies both find group means of 10, 12 and 14. In study A people inside each group vary by about 1 point; in study B by about 15. Which gives the bigger F?
Study A. The between-group piece is identical in both, but study A’s noise is tiny, so the same gap is far more impressive against it. F is a ratio, so shrinking the denominator raises it.
Your F comes out at 0.4. What does that say?
The group means are even closer together than the within-group noise would predict. That is perfectly consistent with H₀ — the lecture notes that an F below 1 is “also ok”. It is certainly not evidence against H₀.
Lecture 10 of 16 · the test · slides 41–42

📈 The F statistic, and the family of F distributions

One ratio: signal over noise

Everything so far has been preparation. F is the one number the whole analysis is built to produce.

  • 🧮F = MS Between ÷ MS Within. Systematic variation on top, noise underneath.
  • 📣It says how much more the group means differ than you would expect under H₀, GIVEN the within-group variation.
  • 1️⃣If H₀ is true, F should be approximately 1. Smaller than 1 is also fine.
  • 🚫A large F indicates H₀ is not true.
  • 👥F can never be negative: both mean squares are sums of squares over positive df.
F = MS Between ⁄ MS Within = 280.71 ⁄ 34.89 = 8.05
🪝 Memory hook
F ≈ 1 means nothing is happening. F ≫ 1 means something is.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“F is a ratio: how far apart the group averages are, divided by how noisy people are within a group. If it is about 1 the gaps are ordinary. If it is 8, they are not.”
★ Key insight
The t distribution needs one df; the F distribution needs two, and in that order — numerator first. Quote F as F(2, 54) = 8.05, never as a bare number.
🎬 Watch: The F statistic, and the family of F distributions🎬 Watch: a worked example
🧩 Comprehension check
An ANOVA gives MS Between = 48 and F = 6.0. What is MS Within?
48 ÷ 6.0 = 8. Any one of the three can be recovered from the other two.
Why does the F distribution need two degrees of freedom when t needs only one?
Because F is built from two independent estimates of variance, and each one has its own df. The shape of the distribution depends on both.

📊 From F to a p-value — reading the table

Reading the table, and the tail that matters

The p-value is defined as the right-tail probability of the calculated F, if H₀ is true. Because large F is the evidence, only the upper tail counts.

  • 📑Enter the table with the PAIR (df numerator, df denominator) = (k − 1, N − k), in that order.
  • 🔎For the Mozart study that is (2, 54). The table has no row for 54, so the lecture uses (2, 50) — the next lower df, which is the cautious direction.
  • ⚖️Compare your observed F with the critical values in that row.
  • 🎯Observed F = 8.05 is past even the .001 critical value of 7.96, so p < .001.
  • ✅Reject H₀ when the observed F is LARGER than the critical value — equivalently when p < α.
🪝 Memory hook
Right tail only. Big F, small p.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“The p-value is the chance of getting an F this big if the groups really were all the same. Here it is under one in a thousand, so ‘they are all the same’ is not a comfortable story.”
★ Key insight
The F test is one-tailed on the right even though the alternative hypothesis has no direction. Squaring has already removed the signs, so every departure from H₀ — in any direction — pushes F upward.

📋 The complete ANOVA table

Three rules fill every cell, forwards or backwards

Every quantity in the lecture lives in one small table, and the table obeys only three rules. Learn the rules and you can fill any version of it, forwards or backwards.

  • ⬇️Rule 1 — SS and df ADD down the column: Between + Within = Total.
  • ➡️Rule 2 — MS = SS ÷ df, on the same row.
  • ➗Rule 3 — F = MS Between ÷ MS Within.
  • 🔄Backwards is the same rules rearranged: SS = MS × df, and df Within = N − k recovers N when only k and the table are given.
  • 🎁Two more quantities come free from the same table: R² = SS Between ÷ SS Total, and the pooled standard deviation sₚ = √(MS Within).

Lecture 13 of 16 · interpretation · slides 44

💪 Effect size: R², or eta-squared

How much of the spread the factor accounts for

A significant F tells you a difference is there. It says nothing about how big it is — and with a large enough sample, a trivial difference can be significant. R² answers the other question.

  • 📐R² (also written η²) = SS Between ÷ SS TOTAL. The denominator is always the total.
  • 📊It is the proportion of variance in the dependent variable that can be attributed to the factor, and it runs from 0 to 1.
  • 🗣️For the Mozart study: 561.41 ÷ 2445.37 = 0.23.
  • 💪Say it in a sentence: of all the variance in spatial awareness scores, 23% is explained by variation between the sound groups.
  • ⚖️In this course, R² above about .20 counts as a strong effect.
R² (η²) = SS Between ⁄ SS Total = 561.41 ⁄ 2445.37 = 0.23
🪝 Memory hook
p says whether. R² says how much.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“The test says the groups really do differ. The effect size says how much of the variation in scores that difference actually accounts for — here about a quarter of it, which is a lot.”
★ Key insight
They are not interchangeable and they are not independent either: p depends on the effect size AND the sample size, while R² depends only on the effect. A tiny R² with p < .001 means a real but small effect measured on a lot of people.
🧪 Demo 5 — significance and size are different questions

🧩 Comprehension check
An ANOVA gives SS Between = 150 and SS Within = 450. What is R²?
SS Total = 150 + 450 = 600, so R² = 150 ÷ 600 = 0.25. Not 150 ÷ 450 — the denominator is the TOTAL.
A study reports p < .001 and R² = .04. How would you summarise it?
A difference that is almost certainly real, but small: the factor accounts for about 4% of the variance. Most likely a large sample made a modest effect detectable.

🧾 The decision, the conclusion, and the causality question

What you may say, and what the design will not let you say

The last line of an ANOVA question is usually a sentence, not a number — and the marks are in how carefully it is worded.

  • ⚖️p < .05, so reject H₀.
  • ✍️The conclusion, in words and about the POPULATION: the average spatial awareness scores for the Mozart, Michael Jackson and Weather-report groups are NOT equal.
  • 🧘Not “all three differ”, and not “Mozart is best”. Just: not all equal.
  • 💪R² > .20, so a strong effect.
  • 🔬Causality is a question about the DESIGN, not about F. Here the independent variable (sound) was MANIPULATED by the researcher and the subjects were RANDOMLY ASSIGNED to the three conditions — so this is a true experiment and a causal claim is allowed.
🪝 Memory hook
Two questions for causality: was it manipulated, and were people randomly assigned?
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“The test says the groups differ. Whether the music CAUSED it depends on how the study was run — here they chose who heard what by chance, so yes. If people had picked their own music, no.”
★ Key insight
No F value, however enormous, can turn an observational study into an experiment. Degree of control is decided before any data are collected.
🧪 Demo 6 — may you claim a cause?
🎬 Watch: The decision, the conclusion, and the causality question🎬 Watch: a worked example
🧩 Comprehension check
Students’ exam scores are compared across three degree programmes they chose themselves; F is significant. What may you conclude?
That the population mean scores differ across the programmes — but not that the programme caused it. Self-selected groups may differ in many other ways, so this is observational, and no causal claim is justified.
Fifty plots of land are randomly assigned to three fertilisers and yields compared. F is significant. Now what may you conclude?
Here the factor was manipulated and assignment was random, so a causal claim IS allowed: the fertiliser affects yield, and at least one of the three differs from the others. The claim stays modest about WHICH.
Lecture 15 of 16 · the follow-up · slides 48–56

🔀 Post-hoc tests, and the multiple-comparisons problem

Which pairs differ, and what asking costs

H₀ is rejected, so at least two population means differ. Which pairs? The obvious answer — run a t test on each pair — carries a problem the lecture spells out arithmetically.

  • 🔗With three groups there are three pairs: Mozart vs Michael, Mozart vs Weather, Michael vs Weather. In general k(k − 1)/2.
  • 🎲Each individual t test has a 5% Type I error rate, so a 95% chance of a correct decision.
  • 🛡️The chance of being right on all three is 0.95 × 0.95 × 0.95 = 86%, so the risk of at least one false alarm has grown from 5% to about 14%. (Strictly this product assumes the three tests are independent; they share the same data, so 86% is an approximation — the lecture makes the same simplification.)
  • 🧮Bonferroni’s fix: alpha per test = overall α ÷ number of tests = .05 ÷ 3 = .0167. Each test is then 98.33% safe, and 0.9833³ ≈ 95% — the family-wise rate is brought back to at most 5%.
  • ⚠️Mechanically, the comparisons are variants of the two-sample t test. They use the pooled standard deviation sₚ = √MSE and the pooled degrees of freedom DFE, and a pair is declared different when |t_ij| ≥ t**.
t_ij = (x̄ᵢ − x̄ⱼ) ⁄ [ sₚ √(1/nᵢ + 1/nⱼ) ] with sₚ = √(MS Within), df = df Within
🪝 Memory hook
Every extra comparison is another lottery ticket for a false positive.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“If you run lots of tests, sooner or later one of them says ‘significant’ purely by chance. Bonferroni makes each test stricter so that the whole set still has only a 5% chance of fooling you.”
★ Key insight
Bonferroni does not make the analysis better or more sensitive. It makes it HARDER, on purpose, in exchange for a controlled false-alarm rate across the whole family of comparisons.

🧩 Comprehension check
Five groups. How many pairwise comparisons, and what is the Bonferroni alpha for an overall α = .05?
k(k − 1)/2 = 5 × 4 ÷ 2 = 10 pairs, so α per test = .05 ÷ 10 = .005. Note it is the number of PAIRS, not the number of groups, that divides alpha.
Post-hoc comparisons give a minimum significant difference of 6.5, and the means are A = 20, B = 25, C = 32. Which pairs differ?
B − A = 5, which is under 6.5 → not distinguishable. C − A = 12 → differ. C − B = 7 → differ. So C stands apart from both, while A and B cannot be told apart.

✅ Assumptions, and the t test side by side

Three assumptions, and the t test side by side

Two pieces of small print that the exam treats as full-sized questions: what has to be true for the ANOVA to be valid, and how the whole thing lines up against the t test you already knew.

  • 🔔Assumption 1 — the population scores are normally distributed.
  • ⚖️Assumption 2 — the groups have the same variance IN THE POPULATION. It does not mean the sample variances come out identical. (The slides stop there. The rule of thumb you will be asked to apply — largest sample standard deviation no more than twice the smallest — comes from the textbook rather than from this lecture.)
  • 🔒Assumption 3 — the observations are independent.
  • 🧍🧍Equal group SIZES are NOT an assumption. Unbalanced designs are perfectly ordinary.
  • 🟰Against the t test: same quantitative dependent variable, same qualitative factor, same between-subject design. The t test takes exactly 2 groups, the ANOVA takes 2 or more.
🪝 Memory hook
Normal, equal variances, independent. Three assumptions, and group size is not one.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“The test assumes the scores are roughly bell-shaped, that the groups are about equally spread out, and that the measurements do not influence each other. If the spreads are wildly different — more than a factor of two — the test is not safe.”
★ Key insight
Questions about assumptions nearly always hand you the standard deviations and then tell you the plots look Normal. That is a signal: the normality half is settled, so the answer turns on the variance ratio.

🏛️ Synthesis — the whole lecture in one paragraph

Here is the single thesis that ties the whole lecture together: a difference between group means only means something relative to how much people differ inside the groups. Everything else is bookkeeping in the service of that one comparison. The total spread is split in two — between the group means, and within the groups — because the within piece is the one the factor cannot touch, which makes it an honest yardstick. Each piece is divided by its own degrees of freedom so that two sums built from different amounts of data become comparable averages, and the ratio of those averages is F. Under H₀ both estimate the same population variance, so F sits near 1; a large F says the means are further apart than the noise allows, and the table turns it into a p-value. What you win is deliberately small — at least one population mean differs — so R² is reported alongside to say how much of the spread the factor accounts for, post-hoc tests with a corrected alpha are needed to say which pairs differ, and causality is settled by the design rather than by the size of F. Learn the ratio and the rest of the lecture is detail.

Question 15 · lecture 15
Multiple-comparison procedures are variants of the two-sample t test. Which quantities do they borrow from the ANOVA?
  • a.SS Between and the between-groups degrees of freedom
  • b.the overall mean and the total number of observations N
  • c.the standard deviation of the largest group and its own df
  • d.the pooled standard deviation sₚ = √MSE and the pooled df, DFE
Each pairwise t statistic uses the pooled within-group estimate of spread from the ANOVA table, with its degrees of freedom — which is why the ANOVA has to be run first.
The other options
✗a. The between row plays no part in a pairwise comparison.
✗b. Neither quantity appears in the t statistic for a pair.
✗c. Using one group throws away the information in all the others; the estimate is pooled.

Question 20 · lecture 16
A one-factor ANOVA is run on exactly two groups. Which statement is true?

  • b.the ANOVA and the t test can reach different conclusions, since F is two-sided

With two groups the one-factor ANOVA and the pooled-variance two-sample t test are the same test written two ways: F = t², and the p-values match exactly.
The other options
✗a. Two groups is fine; the ANOVA simply adds nothing over the t test there.
✗b. They agree exactly against a two-sided t test. Squaring removes the sign, it does not change the decision.
✗c. Identical tests cannot differ in power.
